Fengdu Ghost City, China — the Town Built for the Dead

丰都鬼城

Deep in China • Folk Story

Published: August 2026

Most cities are built for the living. Fengdu was built for the dead — and somehow, you can now buy a ticket and walk right through it.

Perched on Ming Mountain, on the north bank of the Yangtze River in Chongqing, Fengdu Ghost City is a sprawling complex of temples, shrines, and monasteries devoted to one subject: what happens after you die. For nearly two thousand years, legend says, this place has been imagined as a working map of the Chinese underworld — a stone-and-timber model of hell, judgment, and the thin line between the virtuous and the wicked.

To a first-time foreign visitor, the effect is uncanny. There are no somber cemeteries here, no quiet mourning. Instead there are painted demons with bulging eyes, bridges you are told only the good can cross, and a palace where the dead must stand on one foot for three minutes to prove they deserve another life. It is, in the most literal sense, a theme park for the afterlife — and the Chinese have been running it for centuries.

A Capital of the Underworld, Built on a Mountain

Fengdu is designed to resemble Youdu, the mythical capital of Diyu — the Chinese underworld. The complex blends two traditions: the older Diyu of Chinese folk belief and Naraka, the Buddhist hell absorbed into Chinese culture over the centuries. Together they produce a very specific vision: the dead do not simply disappear. They travel, they are judged, and they are punished or rewarded according to how they lived.

Walk the grounds and you will see dioramas and statues acting out the consequences. Sinners, in the carved reliefs, are shown receiving the penalties legend assigns them — a visual curriculum in morality that doubles as tourist spectacle. For locals this is devotion; for a Western eye it reads like a haunted house with a philosophy degree.

The Three Tests

The heart of Fengdu is a sequence of three "tests" the dead must pass before moving on. They are laid out as physical stations you can walk, and today performers in demon costumes reenact the judging for visitors.

The first is the Bridge of Helplessness (Naihe Bridge), a stone bridge from the Ming dynasty with three arches — though only the middle arch counts. Tradition says the rules for crossing depended on who you were: your sex, age, and whether you were married all changed how you would be judged. Demons stood watch; the virtuous crossed safely, the wicked were shoved into the water below. Stand on that middle arch today and you are standing where, in belief, souls were sentenced.

The second test happens at Ghost-Torturing Pass, where the dead face Yanluo Wang — the King of Hell himself. The site is guarded by huge sculptures of demons, all teeth and wrath, frozen mid-judgment. Yanluo appears across Chinese folklore as the ultimate accountant of a life, and here he has a headquarters.

The third test sits at the gate of Tianzi Palace, the largest and oldest building in the complex (about 300 years old). The dead had to balance on one foot on a marked stone for three minutes. Legend holds the good managed it; the wicked toppled and were condemned. It is a quietly brilliant piece of folk psychology — a test of stillness and worth, staged in stone.

Why a Hell Became a Holiday

What makes Fengdu strange to outsiders is that it is not feared. It is a destination.

River cruise ships on the Yangtze pull into its docks regularly. Visitors are driven partway up the mountain, then take an outdoor escalator — yes, an escalator to the underworld — or climb the rest on foot. The Last Glance to Home Tower, built in 1985, marks the spot where the dead are said to look back at their families one final time before entering the next world. It is melancholy and oddly tender, the one moment in this judgment machine given over to love rather than verdict.

This is the part that does not translate through a dictionary. In the West, hell is usually something to avoid mentioning. In China, the afterlife is woven into everyday life — ancestor reverence, the festivals for the departed, the quiet belief that how you treat people now echoes after you are gone. Fengdu makes that belief walkable. You do not have to believe in demons to feel the weight of a culture that built an entire city to remind itself: live well, because someone is counting.

The Characters Behind the Gates

Fengdu is only the entrance to a much larger cast. The Chinese underworld runs its own bureaucracy: Yanluo Wang the judge; Meng Po, who serves the soup that erases your memory before rebirth; Zhong Kui, the demon-queller who catches wandering evil spirits; and the black-and-white Wuchang, the messengers who fetch the dead. To Chinese audiences these are not monsters in a film — they are inherited characters, the mythological furniture of a civilization that has been telling the same stories for two thousand years.

That is why a place like Fengdu survives. It is not a ruin. It is a living piece of how a culture imagines the end — part temple, part theme park, part moral lesson etched in stone.

If You Go

Fengdu sits on the Yangtze between Chongqing and the Three Gorges, which makes it a natural stop on a river cruise. Give it half a day: climb (or ride) the mountain, cross the middle arch of the Bridge of Helplessness, and end at the Last Glance to Home Tower. Watch the demon performers, read the carved punishments, and notice how unafraid everyone is.
