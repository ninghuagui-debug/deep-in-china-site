Yingge Dance, China — the 'Warrior Dance' That Went Viral

英歌舞

Deep in China • Folk Story

Published: August 2026

Most dances ask you to watch. This one looks like it is about to start a war.

In the villages of Chaoshan, a coastal region of Guangdong in southern China, hundreds of men line up in the street, paint their faces into the heroes of a 700-year-old novel, and begin swinging short wooden batons at each other in perfect, thunderous unison. To outsiders who first saw it on a phone screen, there was only one word: warriors. The Chinese internet calls it "战舞" — the warrior dance. The rest of the world, watching it go viral, simply stared.

This is Yingge. And it is one of the most electric things happening in Chinese folk culture right now.

A Battle Staged in a Village Square

Yingge (英歌, sometimes written Yangge) is a folk square dance born in the mid-to-late Ming dynasty, somewhere in the Chaoshan area around modern Shantou, Puning, Huilai, and Lufeng. It blends three things that southern Chinese villagers already loved: Southern-style martial arts, the gestures of Chinese opera, and the stories of the novel *Water Margin* (水浒传) — the tale of 108 outlaws who camped on Mount Liang and fought a corrupt world.

So a Yingge troupe is not really "dancing." It is performing a rebellion in formation.

The performers take on the faces of Liangshan's heroes. The red-faced, red-bearded "First Hammer" leads as Qin Ming or Guan Sheng; the black-faced "Second Hammer" is the wild Li Kui; the "Snake Dancer" plays the sneak Shi Qian, weaving through the ranks with a cloth snake; the drummer sits at the heart of it all as Song Jiang, the outlaw chief. There are no auditions for these roles — every child in the village knows who is who.

The Stick That Holds the Whole Thing Together

The soul of Yingge is a simple object: the *yingge cudgel* (英歌槌), a short wooden baton about 35 to 58 centimeters long, painted black and red with a Taiji symbol at each end. Performers hold one in each hand and strike them together — click, click, CLACK — in rhythms set by the drums.

What looks like chaos is actually a vocabulary. Slow-tempo Yingge uses longer cudgels (around 60 cm) and a heavy, ancient feel; it is thought to be the oldest form. Medium-tempo tightens the strikes into sets of five, seven, even thirteen moves drawn from Southern Fist stances. Fast-tempo — the shortest cudgels, about 40 cm — is pure velocity, a blur of wood and sweat that has become the version the internet cannot stop replaying.

A troupe's size is never random. It is built from the men of the village, always an even number, never fewer than 16 and never more than 108 — the sacred count of Liangshan's heroes. Most commonly you will see 36 (the "Thirty-Six Heavenly Spirits") or 72 (the "Seventy-Two Earthly Fiends").

Painted Like Demons, Moving Like Soldiers

The makeup is part opera, part totem. Two styles dominate: ghost-face makeup in stark black and white, and opera-face makeup borrowed from *Water Margin* characters, each designed to match a hero's temperament. Performers tie a tight black band around the head to pull the skin flat, then paint in a fixed order — black first, then white, then color. Many grow full beards for the rugged look; some go shirtless and paint patterns straight onto their chests.

Behind the faces, the music is nothing but rhythm. No melody, no singer — only a percussion team of bass drums, gongs, cymbals, and a conch horn blasting above the square. The dancers change formations — circles, snakes, two armies closing in — driven entirely by the drum.

From Village Ritual to National Treasure

For centuries Yingge was a Lantern Festival street performance, a local spectacle at the edge of the rice fields. That changed on May 20, 2006, when it was inscribed in the very first batch of China's National Intangible Cultural Heritage — one of just 518 items the state pledged to protect. Regional styles were later recognized too: Chaoyang Yingge, Puning Yingge, Jiazi Yingge, and more, each with its own inheritors trained to pass the form to the next generation.

Why the World Suddenly Noticed

Yingge had always been a southern secret. Then short video arrived. Clips of hundreds of painted men striking in unison — faces fierce, batons a blur, drums like gunfire — began circulating far beyond Guangdong. Viewers compared it to everything from *300* to *The Matrix*. Comment sections filled with the same surprise: "This is a dance?" "This is an army." "This is the most hardcore folk art I've ever seen."

What the screen captures is exactly what a village square has known for four hundred years: that a dance can be a weapon of joy, a rehearsal of courage, and a way for a community to remember who it is. In an age of polished, weightless content, Yingge is gloriously heavy — wood on wood, foot on earth, a whole town moving as one body.
